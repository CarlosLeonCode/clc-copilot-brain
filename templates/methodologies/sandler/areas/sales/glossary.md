# Sales Glossary

Key words of the Sandler selling method, explained in plain words. Ordered as they show up in a sale: from mindset to close. Add your team's own terms to the group they belong to.

---

## Mindset and routine

| Term | Meaning | Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Success triangle** | The three things a good sale stands on: **attitude** (how you think), **behavior** (your daily routine) and **technique** (how you run the conversation). | Knowing the words by heart is not enough if you skip the day's calls. |
| **A no is a no** | Not every business is for us. A no is not a failure: it frees time for someone who does need you. | If the business already handles the problem well and is happy, do not push. |
| **Go for the no** | Look for the no on purpose to filter fast who does not fit, instead of chasing everyone. | "If you see this does not apply to you, just tell me." |
| **"I don't need this sale"** | The mindset before each call: you talk calmly, without pressure, because you do not depend on this one deal. | Without that pressure you listen better and do not give away a demo "so they can look at it". |
| **Success cookbook** | Your fixed routine of sales activities. Your results depend on keeping it. | Daily blocks for prospecting, calls and follow-ups. |

## The cold conversation

| Term | Meaning | Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **30-second commercial** | What you say at the start of a cold call (30 to 45 seconds). It does not say how good we are: it names the prospect's problems. Three parts: a simple introduction, three pain statements and a hook question. | It replaces the classic elevator pitch. |
| **Pain statement** | One sentence that describes a buyer's problem in three parts: the feeling, the concrete problem and what it costs. It starts with an emotional word (worried, tired, frustrated). | "Many owners are worried because messages that arrive at night go unanswered, and those customers buy somewhere else." |
| **Hook question** | The question that ends your 30-second commercial so the prospect starts talking. | "Does any of this sound familiar?" |
| **70/30 rule** | The prospect talks 70% of the time; you, 30%. | If you are talking more than them, something is off. |
| **Equal business stature** | Talking as an equal, like a consultant, not like someone asking for a favor. | Say "glad we talked", not "sorry to bother you". |
| **Bonding and rapport** | Creating a comfortable, trusting space to do business. | Before asking about their problem, make them feel at ease. |

## The rules of the game

| Term | Meaning | Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Upfront contract** | At the start of every meeting, agree briefly on how long it lasts, what it is for, that both sides may say no, and that the next step gets booked before hanging up if it makes sense. | "If at the end you see it is not for you, tell me freely; I prefer a clear no." |
| **Permission to say no** | Part of the upfront contract: you tell the prospect they can turn you down without guilt. It lowers the pressure and keeps them from disappearing. | Fewer prospects go silent after the presentation. |
| **Process and date to decide** | Knowing how the business decides, who takes part and by when they want it working. | "How do you usually decide something like this, and when would you like it working?" |

## Qualifying: the Sandler submarine

| Term | Meaning | Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Sandler submarine** | The order of the sale, by compartments: bonding → upfront contract → **pain → budget → decision** → presentation → post-sell. You do not move on until the previous one is closed. | Never present or send prices without qualifying pain, budget and decision. |
| **Qualify** | Check whether the prospect really can and wants to buy. Three things are qualified, in this order: pain, budget and decision. | Check every deal against the qualification matrix. |
| **Pain funnel** | Questions that go down three levels: the surface problem → what it costs the business (money or time, per month) → how it affects them personally. | People buy on emotion and justify it with numbers. |
| **Cost the pain** | Put in money what they are losing **today** by not fixing the problem, instead of talking about future gains. The prospect works out the figure, not you. | "How many requests go unanswered in a week? How many were sales? What is one worth?" |
| **Reporter question** | A cold, factual question that stirs nothing. **Avoid it.** | "How many messages do you get a day?" (no). |
| **Opinion question** | A question that asks for their view and stirs emotion. **Use it.** | "How comfortable are you with what happens to the messages that arrive at night?" (yes). |
| **Hold the silence** | After asking, stay quiet and count to ten in your head. Do not finish their answer. | — |
| **Budget tied to the pain** | Talk about budget by linking it to the monthly loss: if they lose X a month, how much makes sense to invest to fix it? | "If you stop losing that each month, how much would it make sense to invest to fix it?" |
| **Go in by helicopter** | Talk with the person who really decides and controls the money, not only with whoever answers the phone. | — |
| **Ask twice** | If your contact says "I'll pass it to my boss", ask a second time for the meeting with the person who decides, using a third-party story. | "Often I explain this to someone on the team, it gets passed on and gets lost on the way. How about we look at it together with [name]?" |
| **Third-party story** | Telling what usually happens with other businesses so the prospect sees the risk without feeling pointed at. | See the example of ask twice. |
| **Their own view** | When the person who decides is out of reach, ask your contact what they would do. The presentation and the close still happen with the person who decides. | "If it were up to you, would you go ahead?" |

## Objections and close

| Term | Meaning | Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Objection** | A reason the prospect gives for not moving forward. | "Send me the information", "we already have a provider", "I need to think about it". |
| **Inoculation** | Bringing up the usual doubts yourself before the presentation (who else needs to see it, how long it takes to start, the price), in a soft way, so the excuse loses its force. | "At this point three doubts usually come up... does any of them apply?" |
| **Reversing** | Answering a question with a question, to find out what is behind it. | Them: "Does it work with my tools?" You: "Yes. Is there something in particular behind the question?" |
| **Negative reverse** | You take half a step back, as if doubting they need you, so that **they** defend why the problem must be fixed. | "If what you have works and the price is fine, it makes no sense to change, or is there something that does not convince you?" |
| **Presentation** | Stage 4: showing the solution only against the pain already confirmed, with the person who decides present. It ends with "Does this solve what you told us?". | Never send a demo "for them to review": book it and do it together. |
| **Post-sell** | After the yes, and before hanging up, ask what could stop it, to settle last-minute doubts. | "Could anything happen that would stop this before we start?" |

## System and follow-up

| Term | Meaning | Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Close the sale or close the file** | Nobody is chased forever. Every conversation ends with a yes, a no, or a next step with a day and time. | Three attempts with no answer on the agreed dates → the file is closed. |
| **Follow-up commitment** | A follow-up with an exact day and time on the calendar. Never a "I'll call you tomorrow" with no time. | "Shall I call you Thursday at 10 or at 4?" |
| **Close the file** | Ending a case that does not move and logging the reason (no pain, no budget, no access to the person who decides, stays with what they have, other). | — |
